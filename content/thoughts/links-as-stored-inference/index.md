Every retrieval system I use is eager to tell me that two notes are similar. Embedding search computes this on demand: hand it a query and it produces, right now, a fresh estimate of relatedness — then throws the estimate away. Ask tomorrow and it computes it again. An explicit link is a different kind of object. When I add a cross-reference from a note on agent memory to a note on cache invalidation, I am not recording that two texts share vocabulary. I am recording that I, on a particular day, having held both ideas in one working context, decided they connect — and usually why. "These two notes are similar" is a measurement. "I once decided these connect" is a memory of an act.

The two objects have opposite economics. Similarity is cheap to compute and pointless to store; it can be regenerated from the artifacts at any time. A judgment of connection is expensive to produce — someone had to read both things and notice something — and nearly free to store: one edge. Writing it down is memoization applied to reasoning itself. And the most valuable links are precisely the ones similarity would never propose: an analogy joins things that live in different embedding neighborhoods, and a refutation records a relation similarity is structurally blind to, since "close in meaning-space" cannot tell *supports* from *contradicts*. I've written separately about [where knowledge should live — inside rich documents or in the topology between them](/thoughts/node-minds-and-link-minds/); this post is about what a single edge actually is.

## Trail blazers and slip boxes

The idea is old, and I want to claim its lineage rather than pretend to invention. In 1945, [Vannevar Bush](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1945/07/as-we-may-think/303881/) complained that indexes impose artificial paths through knowledge — "the human mind does not work that way. It operates by association" — and designed the memex around trails, durable chains of association a reader lays down by hand. He was explicit about where the value sat: "This is the essential feature of the memex. The process of tying two items together is the important thing." And he saw that trails were exchangeable artifacts: encyclopedias "ready made with a mesh of associative trails running through them," and "a new profession of trail blazers, those who find delight in the task of establishing useful trails through the enormous mass of the common record." A trail is cognition performed once and reused by strangers. That is stored inference, described in 1945.

Niklas Luhmann ran the single-user version for decades. His slip box is the centerpiece of the previous post, so here I'll add only the part that belongs to this one: each of the box's cross-references was a filing-time decision, made once, that paid dividends for thirty years. And Luhmann was clear about what all those frozen decisions added up to — ["a kind of secondary memory will arise, an alter ego with who we can constantly communicate"](https://luhmann.surge.sh/communicating-with-slip-boxes), a second memory that, he added, "gets its own life, independent of its author." The box was less an archive of texts than an archive of judgments, some seventy books' worth of them.

## The price of a bridge

Agent-memory research has started attaching numbers to what a frozen judgment is worth. [HippoRAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14831) (NeurIPS 2024) has an LLM extract a knowledge graph at write time, then answers multi-hop questions with a single Personalized-PageRank walk over the stored structure instead of iterating retrieve–read–retrieve at query time. Its abstract prices the trade directly: comparable or better performance than iterative retrieval while 10–30× cheaper and 6–13× faster, with gains of up to 20% on multi-hop QA. The graph does exactly what a memoization table does — the bridges between entities were inferred once, so no question pays for rediscovering them. [A-MEM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12110) (NeurIPS 2025) is the agentic version: a Zettelkasten-inspired memory whose new notes are autonomously linked to related old ones, with reported improvements concentrated on multi-hop questions — where topology, not content, is the bottleneck.

The accounting behind all of this fits on one line:

$$
\mathrm{value}(A \xrightarrow{\;r\;} B) \;\approx\; p_{\text{reuse}} \cdot c_{\text{rediscover}} \;-\; c_{\text{write}} \;-\; c_{\text{maintain}}
$$

In words: a stored link pays off when the probability that the connection will be needed again, times the cost of rediscovering it from scratch, exceeds the cost of writing it down plus the cost of keeping it true. Nearly every historical failure and every current opportunity in linked knowledge is a story about one of those three cost terms.

## Everything before us died of write cost

Here is the uncomfortable part of the lineage: typed-link systems have failed repeatedly for eighty years, and they failed on write cost, not read value. Bush never solved who maintains the trails; his trail blazers were a profession he hoped into existence, and it never appeared. The Semantic Web asked humans to hand-author typed triples at scale, and humans declined. The design-rationale tools of the 1980s, gIBIS among them, found that asking contributors to classify every contribution into issues, positions, and typed links imposed an overhead most would not pay. Nobody doubted that a well-linked mesh was valuable to read; producing one consumed more attention than it returned. The write term ate every one of them.

The genuinely new fact — the one claim in this essay I'd call novel rather than inherited — is that LLMs collapse that write cost. HippoRAG's graph and A-MEM's links are machine-emitted as a side effect of writing memory; no human classifies anything. [Karpathy's llm-wiki gist](https://gist.github.com/karpathy/442a6bf555914893e9891c11519de94f) puts a finger on it: "The tedious part of maintaining a knowledge base is not the reading or the thinking — it's the bookkeeping." And of Bush's memex: "The part he couldn't solve was who does the maintenance. The LLM handles that." I would hedge his second sentence — emission is demonstrably handled; maintenance I come back to below — but the trail blazers finally exist, and they bill in tokens.

Collapsed is not zero, and the honest counterweight comes from the flagship lab itself. [LazyGraphRAG](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag-setting-a-new-standard-for-quality-and-cost/), Microsoft's follow-up to GraphRAG, matches or beats the full system on their benchmarks while deferring all graph construction to query time, indexing at 0.1% of full GraphRAG's cost. Eager link-building is not automatically worth it — HippoRAG's own gains are dramatic where questions decompose along clean entity hops and modest where the hops are fuzzy. The inequality above is workload-dependent, not a law: stored links pay where reuse is high and structure is clean, and nowhere else by default.

## A cached judgment can be wrong twice

There is a second unsolved term. A link is stored inference, and stored inference includes stored error. Recomputed similarity has one virtue links lack: it always reflects the current corpus. A link written last spring asserts a judgment whose premises may since have dissolved — the paper it leaned on retracted, the system it described redesigned, my own view revised. If note A changes and the edge A→B survives untouched, the mesh now serves confident, retrievable falsehood: a cache-invalidation bug in the middle of a mind. And agents are not robust to this — retrieved records [steer their behavior, and bad ones propagate errors forward](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16067) across a deployment's lifetime. A-MEM gestures at the answer by evolving old notes when new memories arrive, but as far as I can tell no system yet has real invalidation semantics for links: nothing marks an edge as premise-dependent, nothing re-derives it when a premise moves. That is the maintenance term the LLM has not yet collapsed — and it is the same problem, wearing graph clothing, as [deciding what deserves to be written at all](/thoughts/memory-write-path/) and [keeping a knowledge base alive against a changing world](/thoughts/living-knowledge-bases/). A mesh of links is a ledger of past judgments, and ledgers need audits.

**What would change my mind:** If query-time link construction in the LazyGraphRAG style matches eagerly stored links even on high-reuse, multi-hop-heavy workloads — the terrain most favorable to storage — then links are worth computing but not keeping, and "stored" is the wrong half of my title. And if longitudinal deployments show LLM-emitted link meshes degrading answer quality over months, with stale edges costing more than saved rediscovery, the memoization framing fails outright: a link would be not a stored inference but a stored liability.
