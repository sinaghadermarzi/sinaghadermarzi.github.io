In 1995 Edwin Hutchins published a paper whose title still reads like a provocation: ["How a Cockpit Remembers Its Speeds"](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~ehutchins/documents/CockpitSpeeds.pdf). During a descent, the approach speeds are not reliably held in any pilot's biological memory. The remembering is done by speed bugs on the airspeed indicator, printed speed cards, and two pilots cross-checking each other — and the system keeps remembering even when the individuals' memories degrade. Hutchins drew the conclusion carefully: "the cognitive properties of such distributed systems can differ radically from the cognitive properties of the individuals who inhabit them." The unit that performs the memory task is not a pilot. It is the cockpit.

I keep returning to that sentence while watching the current argument over multi-agent AI, because we are rerunning a very old debate with new hardware — and the older literature already knows things the newer one keeps rediscovering.

## The collective already thinks

Two human results anchor the claim that the interesting unit is bigger than the individual.

The first is the Tasmania effect. When rising seas cut Tasmania off from mainland Australia, its small, isolated population gradually *lost* technologies its ancestors had possessed — bone tools, cold-weather clothing, fishing — a case Joseph Henrich analyzed (*American Antiquity*, 2004) as a shrinking collective brain: with fewer minds to learn from, complex skills decayed faster than they could be retransmitted. Intelligence, on this evidence, lives partly in the population and its artifacts. Shrink the network and the mind forgets.

The second is [Woolley and colleagues' collective-intelligence factor](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1193147): across 699 people working in small groups, a general "c factor" predicted group performance across diverse tasks — and it was "not strongly correlated with the average or maximum individual intelligence of group members." What predicted it instead was social sensitivity and equality of conversational turn-taking. The active ingredient of group intelligence is interaction structure, not member quality.

I should be upfront that none of this framing is mine, and almost none of it is recent. Minsky's *Society of Mind* made "intelligence from a society of simpler agents" a slogan in 1986; [Kitcher](https://doi.org/10.2307/2026796) formalized the division of cognitive labor in 1990; termites were coordinating through persistent environmental traces — [stigmergy](https://doi.org/10.1162/106454699568700), where "the trace left in an environment by an action stimulates the performance of a next action" — long before anyone wrote a Markdown file; and "Cognitive Ecology" is literally the title of a [2010 Hutchins paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2010.01089.x). The increment I care about is narrower than the frame. If [antagonistic objectives really do produce different kinds of artificial minds](/thoughts/tradeoffs-make-minds/) — different persistent memory-and-tool architectures over the same base model, not different weights — then the live hypothesis is that such minds become most powerful when they consume one another's artifacts. An ecology in the termite sense: coupled through persistent traces, not just conversation.

## The machine evidence cuts both ways

The pro-society column is real. [Mixture-of-Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04692) layered agents so that each consumes all of the previous layer's outputs, and reported that a stack of open-source models scored 65.1% on AlpacaEval 2.0 against GPT-4 Omni's 57.5%. [Anthropic's engineering account of its multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system) reports an orchestrator–worker design (an Opus 4 lead with Sonnet 4 subagents) outperforming a single-agent Opus 4 by 90.2% on an internal browsing eval. But hedge that number the way it deserves: it is a vendor's internal, non-peer-reviewed eval; the system burned roughly 15× the tokens of a chat; and the agents differ in model tier and role, not in accumulated cognitive architecture. It is directional industry evidence, not a measurement of the ecology thesis.

The deflationary column is at least as strong, and I want it inside this post rather than left to critics. ["More Agents Is All You Need"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05120) shows that simple sampling-and-voting with copies of the *same* model reproduces much of the gain attributed to elaborate multi-agent methods — many "society" results are test-time compute in a costume. A [scaling analysis of compound systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02419) finds voting performance is non-monotonic in the number of calls: it rises, then falls. [Self-MoA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00674) reports that aggregating multiple samples of the single best model often beats mixing different models, and that adding weaker models can hurt. [MAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657) hand-annotated over 1,600 execution traces across seven multi-agent frameworks and found failure rates of 41–86.7%, dominated by specification problems (~41.8%) and inter-agent coordination failures (~36.9%). And the makers of Devin published a position piece titled, unambiguously, ["Don't Build Multi-Agents"](https://cognition.ai/blog/dont-build-multi-agents): at current capability, parallel agents fragment context and make conflicting implicit decisions.

So a fair reading of the machine evidence is not "societies win." It is: societies sometimes win, often at brutal token cost, frequently lose to a well-sampled individual, and fail mostly for organizational reasons.

## The coupling is the object

Look at what the two columns agree on. Woolley's human data says member intelligence barely predicts group intelligence; interaction structure does. MAST's machine data says model capability is rarely the failure source; the wiring is. The deflationary results don't refute the ecology framing — they refute *lazy* ecologies, ones whose only design choice is "more agents." Both literatures point the same direction:

$$
\text{Perf}(\text{society}) \;=\; f\big(\,a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n\,;\; \mathcal{C}\,\big)
$$

In words: the performance of a society of minds depends on the members' abilities *and* on the coupling structure $\mathcal{C}$ — who consumes whose outputs, in what form, at what cadence — and the convergent finding, human and machine alike, is that the variance worth studying lives in the second argument.

Even the field's favorite slogan turns out to be a fight about exactly this. [Hong and Page's 2004 paper](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0403723101) — the origin of "diversity trumps ability" — showed that on rugged problems a group of randomly selected, cognitively diverse solvers can outperform the group of individually best solvers, whose heuristics overlap. But [Thompson's 2014 critique in the Notices of the AMS](https://www.ams.org/notices/201409/rnoti-p1024.pdf) argues the theorem is close to true by construction and that the simulations don't support the sweeping interpretation, while [Singer's 2019 reformulation](https://doi.org/10.1086/701074) defends a narrower version in which diversity itself, not randomness, does the work. I read the fight as a warning label: diversity-beats-ability is not a law, it is a family of models with contested assumptions. Even coupling *density* is nontrivial — [Zollman's epistemic-network models](https://doi.org/10.1086/525605) suggest sparse communication preserves the [transient diversity](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-009-9194-6) a community needs to avoid converging early on the wrong answer, a result that itself [holds only in part of parameter space](https://doi.org/10.1086/690717).

Which is why the position I'll defend is deliberately modest. I am not claiming heterogeneous agent societies beat homogeneous scale — today's evidence won't carry that, and any claimed gain must survive compute-matched controls: a single agent with self-consistency, and Self-MoA-style self-diversity. I am claiming that the coupling between cognitive architectures is the right research object, and that it is measurable. The measurable form is an artifact-compatibility matrix — how much architecture B improves when it consumes architecture A's persistent outputs — which deserves its own post: [artifact compatibility](/thoughts/artifact-compatibility/). The way to settle it is the factorial experiment sketched in [growing minds in the lab](/thoughts/growing-minds-in-the-lab/). And the runtime question — which mind should consume whose artifacts for this task, at what cost — is a capability-matching problem, the same one I argued sits at the heart of [the delegation stack](/thoughts/delegation-stack/). Speed bugs, speed cards, and cross-checking pilots were never impressive components. The cockpit was an impressive coupling.

**What would change my mind:** a compute-matched study in which the best homogeneous society — same model, self-consistency or Self-MoA aggregation — matches every architecturally heterogeneous society across diverse long-horizon workloads, and an artifact-compatibility matrix whose off-diagonal entries are statistically indistinguishable from its diagonal. If mixing kinds of minds never beats sampling one good mind harder, the ecology is a metaphor, not a unit.
